Guides — how each calculator works

A calculator you cannot check is just a number on a screen. Each guide below gives the exact formula our tools use, a worked example you can verify with a pencil, and the mistakes people most often make with it.

EMI guide: the reducing-balance formula

EMI stands for Equated Monthly Instalment. On a standard reducing-balance loan you pay the same amount every month, calculated with this formula:

EMI = P × r × (1 + r)n ÷ ((1 + r)n − 1)

Here P is the loan amount, r is the monthly interest rate (annual rate ÷ 12 ÷ 100), and n is the number of monthly payments. Each month, interest is charged only on the balance still outstanding — that is what "reducing balance" means — so the interest part of your EMI shrinks over time while the principal part grows.

Worked example A loan of 1,000,000 at 12% per year for 5 years: r = 0.01 and n = 60. The formula gives an EMI of about 22,244 per month. Over 60 payments you repay roughly 1,334,667 in total, so the loan costs about 334,667 in interest — a third of the amount borrowed, even at a modest rate.
  • A longer tenure lowers the EMI but raises the total interest — often sharply.
  • Compare loans on total repayment, not on EMI alone; a "cheap" monthly payment can hide an expensive loan.
  • Beware flat-rate quotes: a flat rate charges interest on the full amount for the whole term and costs roughly double what the same number suggests on a reducing-balance loan.

Loan guide: amortization and prepayment

An amortization schedule is the loan's month-by-month diary: how much of each payment was interest, how much was principal, and what balance remains. Two facts stand out in almost every schedule. First, the early years are interest-heavy — on a long loan, most of your first payments barely touch the principal. Second, anything extra you pay goes entirely to principal, which reduces every future interest charge.

That is why small prepayments punch above their weight. Extra money paid in year one removes principal that would otherwise have been charged interest for the entire remaining term.

Worked example On a 2,500,000 loan at 18% over 7 years, the EMI is about 52,544. Adding just 5,000 extra per month — under 10% more — clears the loan roughly a year early and saves interest in the hundreds of thousands. Run your own numbers in the Loan & Prepayment calculator above.
  • Ask your lender whether prepayment penalties apply before paying extra.
  • Prepaying the highest-interest debt first (usually credit cards, then personal loans) saves the most.
  • Keep an emergency fund first — money prepaid into a loan is hard to get back in a crisis.

Compound interest guide: interest on interest

Simple interest is paid only on your original deposit. Compound interest is paid on the deposit plus all the interest already earned — which is why growth curves bend upwards instead of running in a straight line.

A = P × (1 + r ÷ m)m × t

A is the final amount, P the starting amount, r the annual rate, m how many times a year interest is added, and t the years. Our calculator adds your monthly deposits on top of this, month by month. More frequent compounding helps slightly, but the two levers that matter far more are the rate and, above all, the time you give it.

Worked example 100,000 at 10% per year, compounded yearly, for 10 years grows to about 259,374 — the interest (159,374) ends up larger than the original deposit. Leave it 20 years and it becomes about 672,750: the second decade adds more than twice as much as the first.
  • The Rule of 72 estimates doubling time: 72 ÷ annual rate. At 9%, money doubles roughly every 8 years.
  • Starting 5 years earlier usually beats depositing more later.
  • Real returns vary year to year and can be negative; a calculator's smooth curve is a planning tool, not a forecast.

Savings guide: turning a goal into a monthly habit

A savings goal is just three numbers: the target, what you already have, and what you can set aside each month. The calculator runs those numbers forward month by month — adding your deposit, then applying one month of growth — until the balance crosses the target. If you instead fix the date, it works backwards to the monthly amount that date demands.

Two practical lessons fall out of the maths. First, the required monthly amount climbs steeply as the deadline shrinks, because there are fewer months for growth to help. Second, most goals fail not on the amount but on interruptions — which is why automating the transfer for the day after payday works better than saving "whatever is left".

  • Name the goal and keep it in a separate account so it does not quietly get spent.
  • Raise the deposit with every pay rise, even by a small percentage.
  • For goals under a year away, favour safety over return — a market dip at the wrong moment costs more than the interest earns.

The 50/30/20 budget: a starting point, not a law

Divide after-tax income into needs, wants and savings — then adjust it to the city and family you actually live in. Read article

The 50/30/20 rule is the most quoted budgeting framework in personal finance: spend roughly 50% of your after-tax income on needs (rent, groceries, utilities, transport, minimum loan payments), 30% on wants (eating out, entertainment, subscriptions, upgrades), and 20% on savings and extra debt repayment. Its virtue is not precision — it is that you can apply it in five minutes with one bank statement.

How to apply it honestly

Start by sorting last month's real spending, not the spending you wish you had done. Most people discover their "needs" are quietly padded with wants: the premium grocery brands, the second car, the data package three sizes too big. That is not a failure — it is exactly the information the exercise exists to produce. Move one item at a time rather than attempting a perfect budget in a single month.

When the numbers do not fit

In high-rent cities, needs can swallow 65–70% of income, and no amount of discipline fixes arithmetic. In that case treat 50/30/20 as a direction rather than a destination: the useful target becomes the savings slice. Even moving from 3% to 8% saved transforms what the Savings Goal calculator says about your future. Conversely, if your needs are genuinely low, push savings well past 20% — the rule is a floor for saving, not a ceiling.

The part people skip

"Savings" in the 20% includes debt prepayment above the minimum, because interest avoided is a guaranteed return. Paying extra against an 18% loan beats earning 10% on a deposit every single time. Work through your debts with the Loan & Prepayment calculator before deciding where the 20% goes.

Emergency fund: how much is enough, and where to keep it

Three to six months of expenses is the standard answer — but the right number depends on how replaceable your income is. Read article

An emergency fund is not an investment. It is insurance you hold yourself, and its job is to stop a bad month — a lost job, a medical bill, a car that dies — from becoming expensive debt. Every financial crisis that ends in a high-interest loan began as a smaller crisis that had to be borrowed through.

Sizing the fund

The standard guidance is three to six months of essential expenses — not income: rent, food, utilities, transport and minimum payments. Lean towards three months if you have a stable salaried job, a working partner, and family you could fall back on. Lean towards six or more if you are self-employed, the sole earner, work in a shrinking industry, or have dependants with health needs. Freelancers with irregular income should also smooth the definition: build the fund from your average month, and let good months top it up.

Where it should sit

The fund must be boring and reachable: an ordinary savings account, or a money-market style account you can withdraw from within a day or two. It should not be in stocks (which fall exactly when jobs disappear), not locked in a fixed deposit with early-withdrawal penalties, and not lent to relatives. The interest it earns is a minor bonus; use the Compound Interest calculator if you want to see it, but do not chase return with this money.

Building it without misery

Set a first milestone of one month of expenses — reachable fast enough to keep motivation alive — then automate a transfer for the day after payday. After any withdrawal, refilling the fund becomes the top savings priority until it is whole again. Use the Savings Goal calculator with your target and monthly amount to get a realistic date.

Prepay the loan or invest the spare cash?

The maths compares a guaranteed return against an uncertain one — and your sleep is a legitimate input. Read article

Every month some households face a happy problem: a little money left over. Two good uses compete for it — pay down a loan early, or invest. The comparison starts with a simple observation: prepaying a loan earns you exactly the loan's interest rate, guaranteed and tax-free. Prepaying an 18% personal loan is an 18% risk-free return. Almost no investment offers that honestly.

When prepaying wins

  • The loan rate is higher than the return you could realistically and safely earn — true for credit cards and most personal loans.
  • The loan is in its early years, when balances (and therefore interest charges) are largest.
  • Being debt-free would change your decisions for the better — taking a better job, starting a business, sleeping properly.

When investing can win

  • The loan is cheap (a subsidised mortgage or low-rate auto loan) and the term is long, while your investment horizon is also long.
  • Your employer or a government scheme adds matching contributions or tax relief that beats the loan rate.
  • You already hold a full emergency fund, so the invested money will not be needed in a hurry.

Run both sides before deciding: the Loan & Prepayment calculator shows the interest a given extra payment saves, and the Compound Interest calculator shows what the same monthly amount might grow into. Compare the guaranteed saving with the expected — never promised — investment growth, and be honest about which risk you are actually comfortable carrying.

Flat rate vs reducing balance: the 2× trap in loan ads

A "10% flat" loan can cost more than a 17% reducing-balance loan. Here is how to spot the difference before you sign. Read article

Two loans can both advertise "10%" and cost wildly different amounts, because the percentage is calculated on different things. On a reducing-balance loan, interest each month is charged only on what you still owe. On a flat-rate loan, interest is charged on the original amount for the entire term — even in the final month, when you have almost repaid it.

A concrete comparison

Borrow 300,000 for 3 years. At 10% flat, total interest is 300,000 × 10% × 3 = 90,000, and the monthly payment is (300,000 + 90,000) ÷ 36 = 10,833. At 10% reducing balance, the EMI from our EMI calculator is about 9,681 and total interest is roughly 48,500. Same headline rate — the flat loan costs nearly double. As a rough conversion, a flat rate behaves like a reducing-balance rate of about 1.8–1.9 times as much.

Questions to ask any lender

  • "Is this rate flat or reducing balance?" If the answer is evasive, assume flat.
  • "What is the total amount I will repay over the full term?" Compare loans on this number.
  • "Are there processing fees, insurance bundles or prepayment penalties?" A fee of 2–3% of the loan can quietly add the equivalent of several points of interest on a short loan.
  • "What happens if I miss a payment?" Late fees and penalty interest rates matter more than the brochure rate for many borrowers.

Whenever a lender gives you a monthly payment and a term, you can reverse-engineer the truth yourself: total repayment = payment × months, and interest = total repayment − amount borrowed. If that interest looks like about double what our EMI calculator shows for the quoted rate, you are looking at a flat-rate loan.

Frequently asked questions

The questions readers ask us most about these calculators and the loans and savings behind them.

How is an EMI calculated?

EMI = P × r × (1 + r)n ÷ ((1 + r)n − 1), where P is the loan amount, r is the monthly interest rate (annual rate divided by 12, then by 100), and n is the number of monthly payments. The formula is derived so that equal payments exactly retire the loan, with interest charged each month on the shrinking balance. Our EMI guide walks through a complete worked example.

Why does a longer loan tenure cost so much more?

Because interest is charged every month on the outstanding balance, and a longer tenure keeps that balance high for longer. Doubling a loan's term rarely doubles the interest — it often more than doubles it, because in the early years of a long loan almost all of each payment is interest and the balance falls very slowly. A lower EMI is bought with a much higher total cost; compare the "total you repay" figure, not just the monthly amount.

Is it better to reduce my EMI or reduce my tenure after a part-payment?

If you can afford the current EMI, reducing the tenure saves far more interest, because the balance is extinguished sooner and fewer monthly interest charges ever occur. Reducing the EMI keeps the term (and the interest meter) running just as long, only slightly slower. Choose the EMI reduction only when cash flow is genuinely tight — it is payment relief, not a saving strategy.

What is a realistic rate of return to use in the compound interest calculator?

Use the rate that matches where the money actually sits: a bank savings account rate for a savings account, a term-deposit rate for a deposit, and for long-term diversified investments a cautious figure below historical averages — many planners model 8–12% per year for equity-heavy portfolios over decades, while stressing that any single decade can be far worse or better. If a scheme promises a high, guaranteed, fixed return with no risk, that is a warning sign, not an opportunity.
